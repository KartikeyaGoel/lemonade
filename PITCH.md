# Lemonade — the pitch

## The version you say out loud

Four beats. Nothing to explain, no comparison to anything.

> **"Every money app for kids is a quiz.**
>
> **But a kid can ace a quiz about saving and still drop $40 on Robux.**
>
> **So we don't quiz them. We give them a lemonade stand and let them lose
> money on it. They charge three dollars, they watch people walk away, they
> get undercut by the kid down the street. Then they sell the stand — and
> take that money into the real stock market. Real companies, real prices.
> And every company looks like their lemonade stand, because it is one.**
>
> **You get a page that says what they figured out. Not what they were told."**

Beat two is the whole pitch. Every parent of a ten-to-fourteen-year-old has
lived that sentence. You are not informing them of a problem — you are naming
one they already have. Stop talking after it and let them nod.

### The one sentence, if you only get one

> **"Kids don't need to be told what money means. They need to be given some
> and made to decide."**

### Say the numbers, not the theory

If they want substance, don't reach for a framework. Reach for the screen:

- *"It's real SEC filings. Real prices. Twenty-four companies they've heard
  of — Costco, Chipotle, Roblox, Crocs."*
- *"When they finish, the market goes live. Come back in a month and it says:
  four weeks went by, your money is up twenty-eight dollars, Duolingo's up
  twenty percent, Crocs is down fifteen."*
- *"No accounts. No backend. Nothing leaves the phone. It works on a plane."*
- *"No real money, ever. No leverage, no options, no day trading — those
  teach gambling."*

---

## Pocket answers

Keep these short. Answer, then stop.

**"So it's Duolingo for finance?"**
> "Duolingo works because there's a right answer — *perro* means dog. There's
> no right answer to 'should I charge two dollars or three.' You can quiz a
> kid on Spanish. You can't quiz them on judgment."

**"Isn't this just Greenlight?"**
> "Greenlight makes its money on the card. The learning part is advertising
> for the card. Nobody there gets paid on whether your kid opens it twice."
>
> *(If it's the customer who already told you Greenlight is horrendous: don't
> pitch. Ask her what made it horrendous, and shut up.)*

**"How do I know they're learning anything?"**
> "There's a page for you. It only says things they actually did — held on
> through a twelve percent drop instead of panic-selling, spread their money
> across four companies without being told to. Never a quiz score. We don't
> have quiz scores on purpose."

**"Won't this teach my kid to gamble?"**
> "No day trading, no leverage, no shorting, no options — ever. They buy
> pieces of companies that make things they've eaten or worn, and they have
> to say why before they can buy."

**"Is it fun, though? My kid plays Roblox."**
> "That's the actual bar, and it's the one we're least sure of. Want to sit
> your kid down with it and tell me what happens?"
>
> *(Say this honestly. A parent trusts the person who names their own weak
> spot before being asked.)*

---

## What not to say

- **Don't explain the Duolingo argument unprompted.** It's a defence, not an
  opening. Leading with it makes you sound like you're pitching a thesis
  instead of a thing their kid might like.
- **Don't say "financial literacy."** It's the phrase on every product they've
  already deleted.
- **Don't say "gamified."** Same problem.
- **Don't list the five stages.** Show the screen instead.
- **Don't claim kids love it.** No kid has played it yet. Saying so out loud
  is worth more than the claim would be.

# The long version

Everything below is for an investor, a school, or anyone who pushes back.
Do not read it at a parent.

> Everyone building financial education for kids is building Duolingo.
> Finance is not a language. It is a game.

---

## 1. The one-liner

**A kid runs a lemonade stand until they understand a business from the
inside — then takes the money they made and buys real companies on the real
market.**

The thesis the whole product hangs on: **every stock is somebody else's
lemonade stand.**

---

## 2. The insight: "Duolingo for finance" is a category error

This is the argument the product rests on. It is **not** the argument you
open with — see "What not to say" above. It is what you say when somebody
tells you the category is already solved.

### Why Duolingo works

Duolingo works because of a property of **language**, not a property of
Duolingo:

- **The item count is effectively infinite.** Spanish has tens of thousands
  of words, each an atomic, independently learnable unit. You can never run
  out of content.
- **Recall *is* the skill.** Knowing that *perro* means dog is not a proxy
  for speaking Spanish. It is a component of speaking Spanish. Drilling the
  atom improves the whole.
- **Grading is objective and instant.** There is a right answer. The app
  knows it. The kid knows within 300ms.
- **Progress is real and felt.** 500 words is genuinely more Spanish than
  100 words. The number going up is not a metaphor.

A spaced-repetition quiz engine with streaks is the *correct* architecture
for that problem. Duolingo did not get lucky. They matched the shape of the
tool to the shape of the skill.

### Why the same architecture fails on finance

Finance has none of those four properties.

- **The item count is tiny.** The concepts a 12-year-old needs — revenue,
  cost, margin, price, demand, fixed vs variable cost, capacity, competition,
  ownership, earnings, valuation, diversification, risk, time — is a list of
  about thirty. You exhaust the curriculum in a week and then you are
  padding.
- **Recall is not the skill.** Knowing the *definition* of margin is not a
  component of financial competence. Every kid who fails at money can define
  "budget." The skill is **judgment under uncertainty with your own money on
  the line, over time.** No flashcard has ever contained that.
- **There is no right answer to grade.** "Should you charge $1.50 or $2.00?"
  depends on the weather, the competition, and what you are trying to do.
  A quiz engine cannot mark it, so quiz engines don't ask it — they ask
  "what does P/E stand for?" instead, and that is the entire failure in one
  sentence.
- **Progress is not felt.** Knowing twelve definitions instead of eight does
  not feel like anything. There is no fluency curve to climb.

**So the Duolingo shape, applied to finance, degrades into trivia.** Not
because those teams are lazy — because the architecture forces it. If your
engine grades atoms, you will ship atoms, and finance's atoms are worthless.
The kid learns to pass the quiz. That is a measurable skill, it is just not
the one anybody wanted.

### The evidence is already in the room

**Greenlight is the Duolingo for finance.** Card-linked, quiz-and-badge
learning layer, streaks, lessons. And the verdict from our own first customer
— a parent, paying, with a kid in the target age band — was *horrendous*.

That is not a competitor we have to displace. That is a competitor whose
churned users are our pipeline.

There is also a structural reason Greenlight-style products can't fix this by
trying harder: **they earn on interchange and banking, not on learning.** The
learning layer is customer acquisition for a debit card. Nobody there is
compensated on whether a kid opens it a second time. We are a game company
with a learning thesis; they are a fintech with a content department. That
difference shows up in the product every single time.

### The right analogy is Clash Royale

Ask any twelve-year-old who plays Clash Royale to define "opportunity cost."
They can't. Then watch them refuse to drop a Golem at four elixir because
they'd be naked to a counter-push for eleven seconds.

**They have the skill. They don't have the word.** And they acquired it
without a single lesson, quiz, or explainer video — because the game gave
them a loop with real stakes, immediate consequences, and an opponent that
punished bad judgment.

That is exactly the shape of financial competence:

| | Language (Duolingo) | Judgment (Clash Royale) |
|---|---|---|
| Skill type | Recall | Decision under uncertainty |
| Content | Infinite atoms | Small rule set, infinite situations |
| Teaching | Drill the atom | Put stakes on the decision |
| Feedback | Right/wrong | Consequence |
| Vocabulary | Is the point | Comes after, if at all |
| Retention | Streak guilt | The world moved while you were gone |

**Finance is in the right-hand column. Every product in the category is built
in the left.**

So: we are not building Duolingo for finance. **We are building Clash Royale
for finance** — and then, because the parent is the buyer, we add the one
thing Clash Royale never had to: *we name what the kid learned, and we prove
it.*

## 3. What we actually built

Five stages. Each is a wall the kid hits, and the concept is what gets them
over it. No stage teaches a word before the kid has already done the thing.

| Stage | The kid's question | What it actually is |
|---|---|---|
| **1. One stand** | *What is a cup worth to them?* | Price, cost and margin |
| **2. More stands** | *How do I sell more than my own two hands can?* | Capacity, capital, hiring, competition |
| **3. The shop** | *What do I owe on a day nobody comes?* | Fixed costs, operating leverage, debt against equity |
| **4. Go public** | *What is one piece of my company worth?* | Valuation, ownership, shares, share price |
| **5. Markets** | *Whose business do I want a piece of?* | Public markets, reading a filing |

Stage 1 doesn't tell a kid about demand curves. It lets them charge $3.00 and
watch people walk away. At the end of the week it plots **their own seven
price points** and says: you found this.

Stage 2 doesn't explain why companies hire people. It caps the kid at thirty
cups a day, and the only way past it is to pay somebody to mind the first stand
so they can go and work a second one. *You cannot be in two places at once* is a
thing a child works out, not a thing we say.

Stage 4 is the one that took a customer conversation to see. The kid cuts their
own company into a thousand shares, divides to get the price, sells 30% of them
and keeps running it — and then trades one week as a public company and watches
that price move, with the two numbers that moved it printed beside it. **They
get a share price of their own before they ever see anybody else's.**

So by the market, the kid has priced a product, been undercut by a rival, hired
a manager, owed rent on a day nobody came, chosen between a bank and an
investor, and rung the bell on their own listing. Then they open a stock screen
and see revenue, net income, share count and price — the same four numbers they
have been living inside for an hour. **They are not learning to read a company.
They are recognising one.**

### The data is real

Fundamentals come from **SEC EDGAR 10-K filings**. Prices are **five years of
real weekly closes** across **24 companies a kid has heard of** — Costco,
Chipotle, Nike, Roblox, Netflix, Duolingo, Crocs, Nvidia, Amazon.

No fake dashboards. No mocked charts. **Any two numbers on screen reconcile
with the third on paper.** If we print cost and price, their difference *is*
the margin.

### And then the market opens for real

Finishing the arc unlocks the same market **live** — anchored to this week's
actual closes, with **no button that makes time pass.**

This is the Clash Royale retention mechanic, and it is the only honest one in
the category. A streak is a product manager threatening a child. Our reason
to come back is that **the world genuinely moved.** Come back after four
weeks and the game says:

> *4 weeks went by. 2026-08-03 to 2026-09-01. Nobody pressed anything.
> +$27.78. Duolingo +19.9%. Crocs −15.1%.*

Every one of those numbers is real, and the money is theirs — carried through
from the lemonade stand they sold. **Nobody needs to nag a kid to check what
their money did.**

## 4. The safety line, which is also a design line

**No real money, ever. No day trading, no leverage, no shorting, no options.**

Not a compliance checkbox — a curriculum decision. Those instruments teach
gambling reflexes, and a product that hands a ten-year-old a 3x leveraged ETF
has taught them the opposite of what the parent bought. The universe stays
deliberately narrow: **real, recognisable, operating companies**. A kid can
understand why Chipotle makes money. That is the entire point.

**Privacy is structural, not promised.** No accounts, no backend, no
analytics, no third-party domains. Every save lives in `localStorage` on the
device. The game works with the wifi off. A school can audit this in ten
minutes — and `PRIVACY.md` tells them how.

## 5. The parent side — the part nobody else has

The parent is not buying a game. **They are buying evidence that their kid
understands money.**

So there is a grown-up view, and it is not a report card. It reports
**behaviour, never quiz scores** — because we don't have quiz scores, on
purpose. Every line fills in from something the kid *did*:

- *Held through a 12% drawdown instead of selling.*
- *Spread across four companies without being told to.*
- *Raised price after demand held at $2.00.*

Ten tracked skills, each at *unseen / emerging / held*, grouped by act, with
the concept name the kid never sees. On day zero it says, honestly:

> *Nothing shown yet — they have not started. Every line below fills in from
> something they do, never from something they are told.*

**That last sentence is the whole company.**

---

## 6. Why this is hard to copy

1. **The thesis has to be in the architecture.** You cannot bolt "learning by
   consequence" onto a quiz engine. Greenlight would have to throw away the
   thing they built.
2. **Their economics forbid it.** Interchange revenue means the learning layer
   is a marketing cost. Marketing costs don't get rebuilt from scratch.
3. **Real data is a grind.** SEC XBRL parsing, a shared weekly date axis so a
   market-wide fall lands on every company in the same week, a rolling window
   that doesn't silently corrupt saved accounts. Tedious, unglamorous,
   load-bearing.
4. **The rigour is enforced by machine.** Reading level is checked at build
   (currently **4.8**, and at most 3% of sentences may run over 22 words).
   Contrast is checked at build. **Over 1,100 tests** hold the invariants — including that no jargon can leak into
   a kid-facing string.

## 7. Where it actually stands

**Shipped and working today:**
- Five stages, playable end to end in a sitting, mobile-first
- Real SEC fundamentals + five years of real weekly prices, 24 companies
- Live market anchored to this week, with catch-up on return
- Behavioural mastery tracking and the grown-up view
- Offline-capable, zero backend, zero accounts
- Over 1,100 tests, clean typecheck, clean lint, reading level 4.8

**Honestly not done:**
- **No kid has played it yet.** That is the single biggest open risk and no
  amount of engineering substitutes for it.
- 24 tickers, not a few hundred (needs a data key, not a redesign)
- Weekly price granularity, not daily
- Three of the five stages still lack a closing recap. Act 1 ends on
  `week-end`, a real look back with a chart; the run ends on `finale`. Stages
  2, 3 and 4 hand over through `act-intro`, which introduces what is next
  rather than closing what just happened.
- It is a web prototype. **The product is a phone app.**

## 8. What to fill in before you pitch this

Don't let me write these for you — they need to be yours, and a real investor
will check them:

- **Business model.** Subscription? One-time? Who pays and how much?
- **Market size**, with a real source.
- **Distribution.** Parents are hard to reach and kids can't buy. What is the
  channel — schools, parent communities, app store?
- **The Greenlight claim.** Right now the evidence is one parent saying
  "horrendous." That is a fantastic anecdote and a terrible statistic. Get
  five more.
