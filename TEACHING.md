# Lemonade in a classroom

A single 40-minute lesson in which a class of children measures a demand curve
from their own decisions. No accounts, no logins, no data leaves the room.

**You need:** one device per child (phone, tablet or laptop), a projector, and
the address of the game. Nothing is installed.

---

## The idea

Every child plays the *same week*. Same forecasts, same weather, the same
twenty dollars, the same rival. The simulation guarantees it — the weather in a
week depends only on the code, never on what anybody decides — so when thirty
children get thirty different results, **every bit of the difference is a
decision**.

Each child ends up with two numbers: what they charged, and what they made. You
put those sixty numbers on one chart and the class has measured the
relationship between price and profit. Nobody has to be told that charging more
sells fewer, or that there is a best price in the middle. Their own dots say it.

You do not have to know any economics to run this. The chart does the teaching.

---

## Before the lesson (2 minutes)

1. Open the game on the projector.
2. Tap **For a grown-up** at the bottom of the first screen, then **Teaching a
   class?**
3. A **class code** appears — something like `SKY-2000-01ZA-0XS0`. Write it on
   the board.

Keep this screen open. It is your board for the whole lesson.

> Tap **New week** for a different code. Every code is a different real week of
> weather, so two classes never get the same one unless you want them to.

---

## Run it (25 minutes)

### 1. Hand out the code (2 min)

Children open the game and go to **Friends → Same sky → Or play somebody else's
days**, type the code, and press play.

Say this and nothing else:

> *You have twenty dollars and seven days. Buy lemons, pick a price, and see how
> much you have at the end of the week. You will all get exactly the same
> weather, so nobody has an excuse.*

Do **not** tell them what to charge. The whole lesson depends on them
disagreeing.

### 2. They play (15 min)

Seven days takes ten to fifteen minutes. Expect noise. Useful things to say
while walking round, none of which give anything away:

- *What happened yesterday?*
- *How many people walked off without buying?*
- *How much of that dollar fifty do you actually keep?*

If somebody finishes early, they can play the same week again and try to beat
themselves. That is the single most valuable thing an early finisher can do.

### 3. Collect (5 min)

Ask each child for two numbers: **the price they settled on** and **what they
made for the week**. Type them into the board. Names are optional and are never
shown on the chart.

Twelve results is enough for the shape. Thirty is better.

### 4. Look at it (5 min)

Put the chart up. Ask, in this order:

1. **What shape is that?** (Let them describe it. Someone will say "a hill".)
2. **Why is the left-hand side low? Everyone there sold loads of lemonade.**
   — because they kept almost nothing per cup.
3. **Why is the right-hand side low? They were charging the most.**
   — because almost nobody bought anything.
4. **So where is the best price?** — and now they are pointing at the peak of a
   demand curve they measured themselves.

Then press **Show what the week could have done.** The green line is what that
exact week would have earned at every price. Almost always, the class's dots
trace the same hill just underneath it.

> The gap between the two lines is not a mark. It is waste, and shortfall, and
> days spent finding out. That is what running a business costs.

---

## After (8 minutes)

Good questions, roughly in order of difficulty:

- **Somebody in this room lost money on the same week everybody else made money
  on. What did they do differently?**
- **Two people charged the same and got different answers. How?**
  (Batch size: one of them threw lemons away, or ran out at lunchtime.)
- **If lemons got twice as expensive tomorrow, which way would the hill move?**
  (Right. And it is worth letting them argue about it before you say so.)
- **Real shops know their hill. How do you think they found it?**
  (They tried things. Same as you.)

---

## What each child takes away

Their own copy of the game keeps going after the lesson. It runs on for three
more acts — growing the stand, selling it, and then buying shares in real
companies with real filed accounts — but none of that is needed for this lesson
and nothing in it is homework.

If you want to see what a particular child got out of it, the **For a grown-up**
screen on their device lists what they have *demonstrated*, with the day and the
figures attached: "Day 4: sold all 24 cups with 16 people turned away, then put
the price up to $1.75." Nothing on that list is there because the game told them
something. It is there because they did it, more than once.

---

## Ground rules the software keeps for you

- **No real money is ever involved**, at any point, in any act.
- **Nothing leaves the device.** No accounts, no analytics, no network calls
  from a child's device. The class board lives in your browser and nowhere else.
- **No child is ranked against another by luck.** The board never names a child,
  and the game's own comparison screens split results into decisions rather
  than declaring a winner.
- **No child-to-child free text.** Everything shared travels as a code.

---

## Variations

**Two classes, same code.** Run the identical week with two groups and compare
the two charts. They land on the same hill. That is a more convincing
demonstration of a law than any single class can produce alone.

**Change one thing.** Run the same code twice a week apart and ask the class to
agree a rule beforehand — "nobody charges under a dollar" — then see what it
does to the shape.

**Older students.** After the reveal, ask them to work out the equation of the
line through the *cups sold* rather than the profit. It is a straight line, and
it is the demand curve proper; the hill they measured is that line multiplied by
the margin.
